## 1. Scope

This document covers consumer rights relating to offline purchases, including:

- Products sold at local shops, railway stations, airports and retail stores.
- Packaged products sold above their printed Maximum Retail Price (MRP).
- Additional taxes or charges imposed over MRP.
- Products sold below MRP.
- Loose goods sold by weight or measurement.
- Pricing representations and unfair trade practices.
- Consumer complaint and escalation options.

It does not establish that every expensive product or service is illegal.

## 2. Legal Metrology Act, 2009

**Authority:** Department of Consumer Affairs, Government of India.

**Relevant provision: Section 18 — Declarations on pre-packaged commodities**

Section 18(1) requires pre-packaged commodities to carry the declarations and particulars prescribed under the Act and applicable rules.

**Plain-English meaning:**

Products packaged before the consumer is present must comply with the applicable mandatory package declaration requirements.

**Relevant provision: Section 36 — Penalty for non-standard packages**

Section 36(1) addresses pre-packaged commodities that do not conform to the declarations required under the Act.

**Important distinction:**

Do not automatically cite Section 36 as the direct penalty for every instance of overcharging. Rule 18(2) of the Packaged Commodities Rules is the direct provision addressing sales above retail sale price.

## 3. Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011

### 3.1 Rule 6(1)(e): Retail sale price declaration

The retail sale price must clearly indicate that it is the maximum retail price inclusive of all taxes.

**Plain-English meaning:**

The MRP printed on an applicable packaged commodity includes all taxes.

**Consumer example:**

A packaged product displays MRP ₹100. A retailer should not add GST separately on top of that MRP.

### 3.2 Rule 18(2): Sale above retail sale price

The rule states:

"No retail dealer or other person including manufacturer, packer, importer and wholesale dealer shall make any sale of any commodity in packed form at a price exceeding the retail sale price thereof."

**Plain-English meaning:**

A retailer cannot sell an applicable packaged commodity above its declared retail sale price.

**Example:**

- Printed MRP: ₹20
- Amount charged: ₹30
- Difference: ₹10

This is a potential violation of Rule 18(2).

**Typical scenarios:**

- A shop sells a packaged water bottle above MRP.
- A railway station kiosk sells packaged chips above MRP.
- An airport retail counter charges more than the MRP printed on an applicable packaged product.
- A retailer adds a separate tax to the MRP.

The location of an ordinary retail outlet does not, by itself, permit the retailer to disregard Rule 18(2).

### 3.3 Rule 18(2A): Different MRPs on identical packages

This provision addresses manufacturers, packers and importers declaring different MRPs on identical pre-packaged commodities through restrictive or unfair trade practices.

**Important distinction:**

Do not simplify this into a blanket statement that every location-based price difference is illegal. Its wording specifically concerns the conduct of manufacturers, packers and importers and the relevant restrictive or unfair trade practices.

### 3.4 Amendments and current applicability

The 2011 Rules have been amended over time.

The Department of Consumer Affairs lists amendments dated:

- 24 October 2025
- 2 December 2025
- 13 February 2026
- 2026 Second Amendment
- 29 May 2026
- 21 September 2026

**Production rule:** Use the latest applicable consolidated text and amendments. Do not rely only on the original 2011 notification.

## 4. Understanding MRP in everyday purchases

### Scenario A: Packaged product sold above MRP

Example:

A water bottle has a printed MRP of ₹20. A shopkeeper charges ₹25.

**Legal position:** Potential violation of Rule 18(2).

**Useful evidence:**
- Photograph of printed MRP.
- Purchase receipt or invoice.
- Seller or shop details.
- Date and location of purchase.

### Scenario B: Packaged product sold below MRP

Example:

A product has an MRP of ₹100 but the shop sells it for ₹90.

**Legal position:** MRP is a maximum price, not a mandatory selling price. A lower selling price is generally permissible.

### Scenario C: Packaged product with an extra tax

Example:

MRP is ₹100, and the retailer asks for another ₹18 as GST.

**Legal position:** Generally not permissible because MRP is inclusive of taxes under Rule 6(1)(e).

### Scenario D: Loose goods

Examples:
- Vegetables sold by kilogram.
- Loose rice.
- Products weighed in front of the customer.

**Legal position:** These are not automatically subject to the same printed-MRP requirement as pre-packaged commodities.

The applicable weight, measurement, quantity and pricing declarations must be considered separately.

### Scenario E: Prepared food and beverages

Examples:
- Tea prepared at a railway stall.
- A freshly cooked meal.
- Coffee prepared after ordering.

**Legal position:** Do not automatically apply packaged-product MRP rules to freshly prepared food. Consider the nature of the sale, displayed price, applicable tax rules and consumer protection requirements.

### Scenario F: Packaged beverages served in restaurants or hotels

This involves a separate legal issue concerning the Supreme Court decision in *Federation of Hotel and Restaurant Associations of India v. Union of India*.

**Production restriction:** Do not give a blanket answer that restaurants can always charge above MRP, or that they can never do so. Distinguish retail sale of packaged goods from goods supplied as part of a hospitality service, and retrieve the actual judgment before giving a definitive answer.

## 5. Additional charges

### 5.1 Cooling charges

Example: A packaged cold drink has an MRP of ₹40. The retailer asks for ₹5 extra as a cooling charge.

**Initial legal position:** Where the transaction is a retail sale of a packaged commodity, charging above its retail sale price raises a Rule 18(2) issue.

Do not treat refrigeration as automatically creating a separate entitlement to charge above MRP.

### 5.2 Carry bags and packaging

A carry bag or separately supplied packaging service requires analysis of the circumstances.

Consider:
- Whether the charge was disclosed before purchase.
- Whether the consumer agreed to the charge.
- Whether the retailer misrepresented the charge.
- Whether the charge is compulsory or optional.
- Whether the charge relates to a separate service or is an attempt to increase the price of the packaged commodity.

Do not automatically classify every carry-bag charge as illegal.

### 5.3 Restaurant service charges

CCPA Guidelines dated 4 July 2022 state that:

1. Restaurants must not add service charges automatically or by default.
2. Service charges must not be collected under another name.
3. Service charges must be voluntary and optional.
4. Consumers must not be denied entry or service for refusing to pay.
5. Service charges must not be added to the food bill and subjected to GST as described in the guidelines.

The Delhi High Court upheld these guidelines on 28 March 2025. CCPA enforcement action was also reported in January 2026.

**Consumer action:** Ask the restaurant to remove an automatically imposed service charge and retain the invoice if a dispute occurs.

## 6. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

### Section 2(47): Unfair trade practice

The definition includes specified unfair or deceptive practices, including certain false representations about the price of goods or services.

**Plain-English meaning:**

A seller may face consumer-law scrutiny where pricing is falsely represented or a consumer is deceived about the price.

**Examples to investigate:**
- False price representations.
- Misleading discount claims.
- Deceptive price displays.
- Charging an amount inconsistent with the displayed or agreed price.

**Important:** Do not label every high price as an unfair trade practice. Establish the specific misleading or unfair conduct.

## 7. Where can consumers complain?

### 7.1 National Consumer Helpline (NCH)

**Purpose:** Pre-litigation grievance redressal.

Official contact details:
- Toll-free: 1915
- Alternative number: 1800-11-4000
- WhatsApp: +91 8800001915
- SMS: 8800001915
- Website: https://consumerhelpline.gov.in/

Published call availability: 8 AM to 8 PM, daily except national holidays.

Consumers can submit grievances through the official web portal and other listed channels.

### 7.2 State Legal Metrology Department

Consumers may approach the relevant State Controller or Legal Metrology enforcement authority for suspected violations of the Legal Metrology Act and Rules.

For a complaint, useful evidence includes:
- Product packaging showing MRP.
- Invoice or receipt.
- Seller details.
- Date and location.
- Description of the alleged violation.

Do not invent State-specific contact numbers. Retrieve the relevant official State department details when needed.

### 7.3 Consumer Commissions through e-Jagriti

Official portal: https://e-jagriti.gov.in/

e-Jagriti provides official help documents for:
- Consumer complaint filing.
- Payment flow.
- First appeals.
- Revision petitions.
- Other consumer commission proceedings.

Do not direct users to the old e-Daakhil portal as the current default filing platform.

### 7.4 Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)

The CCPA addresses consumer rights violations, unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements within its statutory powers.

Do not represent it as a substitute for every individual consumer dispute process.

## 8. Evidence checklist

When a consumer reports overcharging, ask them to preserve:

1. Original product packaging.
2. A clear photograph of the printed MRP.
3. Purchase receipt or invoice.
4. Seller name and location.
5. Date and approximate time.
6. Photograph of the displayed price, if applicable.
7. Any communication with the seller.

If the consumer has no invoice, do not automatically tell them that they have no remedy. Explain that evidence may be harder to establish and suggest preserving whatever proof is available.

## 9. NyayaVoice answer-generation rules

When answering a pricing complaint:

1. Identify whether the product was pre-packaged, loose or freshly prepared.
2. Ask for the printed MRP, if applicable.
3. Ask what amount the seller charged.
4. Establish whether the extra amount was tax, a service charge or another fee.
5. Retrieve the relevant legal provision.
6. Explain the legal position in simple language.
7. Identify any uncertainty or exception.
8. Suggest evidence to preserve.
9. Explain the appropriate complaint channel.
10. Never guarantee a refund, penalty, prosecution or successful case.

### Example question

"I bought a water bottle at a railway station. The MRP is ₹20, but the shopkeeper charged ₹30. Is this legal?"

### Expected answer behaviour

- Identify it as a potentially over-MRP sale of a pre-packaged commodity.
- Explain Rule 18(2) of the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011.
- Explain that the printed MRP includes applicable taxes.
- Recommend retaining the bottle or photographing the MRP and keeping the invoice.
- Explain how to approach NCH or the State Legal Metrology Department.
- Avoid making claims about a guaranteed outcome.
